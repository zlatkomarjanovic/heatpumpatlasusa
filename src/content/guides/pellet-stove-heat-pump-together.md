If your pellet stove warms the living room and the bedrooms run on another system, start by deciding which rooms the heat pump needs to serve. A stove heating one room and a heat pump heating the whole house do different amounts of work.

## Compare the rooms you already heat

Write down which rooms the stove keeps comfortable, how many tons you buy, and what you use for the rest of the house. Ask the installer which rooms each proposed head or duct will cover and whether you can close the doors in winter without leaving rooms cold.

If your stove burns three tons a year and you expect to keep using one ton, compare the running cost of replacing **two tons**. At the [pellet comparison’s](/guides/heat-pump-vs-pellet-stove/) example price of $433 per ton, you would avoid **$866** of pellet spending. Under that comparison’s assumptions, replacing the heat from two tons with a heat pump at 22¢/kWh would cost about **$532**, for a difference of **$334** before stove maintenance, backup heat, and installation. Any new rooms and cooling add separate energy use.

## When keeping the stove might make sense

Keep it if it serves a room you use a lot and you are comfortable buying, storing, and handling fuel. Consider dropping it if the heat pump comfortably heats the same room and you would rather skip the loading and cleaning. If you expect the stove to cover a power outage, check your specific unit. Many pellet stoves need electricity to feed pellets and run their fans.

[Calculate the cost of switching](/calculator/), then ask your installer to quote the rooms you want covered. Keep any pellet fuel you still expect to burn in your year-one budget.
