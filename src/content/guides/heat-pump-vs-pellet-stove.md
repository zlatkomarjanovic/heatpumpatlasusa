A pellet stove and a heat pump both heat a home, but they are really answers to different questions. A pellet stove is cheap, cosy, hands-on heat for the room it sits in, as long as you can buy pellets at a good price and do not mind the work. A heat pump is quiet, even, whole-home heating and cooling that you mostly forget about. Picking between them is less about a single cost number and more about how you want to live with the system.

## Running cost: it hinges on two local prices

The comparison swings on your pellet price and your electricity rate, and both are local.

- Where bagged pellets are cheap and electricity is expensive, a pellet stove can deliver heat at a lower cost per unit than almost anything else.
- Where pellets are trucked in at a premium and electricity is moderate, a cold-climate heat pump usually delivers cheaper heat overall.

Because a heat pump moves three to four units of heat per unit of electricity, it competes well even against inexpensive fuels. The [operating cost page](/heat-pump-operating-cost/) puts real state electricity prices behind the heat pump side, and the [heat pump vs oil](/heat-pump-vs-oil/) comparison shows how the same efficiency logic plays out against a delivered fuel.

## Coverage: one room versus the whole house

This is the difference people underestimate. A single pellet stove heats the space it stands in and leaks some warmth next door. It does not evenly heat a whole house. Real whole-home pellet heating means a pellet boiler or furnace, which is a far larger and pricier install with a bulk fuel store.

A heat pump is built to condition the entire home from one system, room by room, and it runs in reverse to cool in summer. If you want even temperatures everywhere and air conditioning, that is a heat pump job, not a stove job.

## Effort: hands-on versus hands-off

A pellet stove is work, and honest buyers should price that in:

- Buying, hauling and storing 40-pound bags of pellets through the winter.
- Filling the hopper every day or two in the coldest stretch.
- Emptying ash and cleaning the burn pot and glass regularly.

A heat pump asks for a thermostat setting and an occasional filter change. Neither is wrong, but they are very different lifestyles, and for many households the low-effort factor outweighs a small running-cost gap.

## Upfront cost and rebates

A pellet stove is a relatively low upfront cost for what it does, and it is genuinely appealing as cheap supplemental heat. A whole-home heat pump costs more to install, but it replaces both your heating and your air conditioning, and it is the technology utilities and states actually put [rebate money](/rebates/) behind. Pellet appliances rarely attract the same incentives, which narrows the upfront gap once you count what is on the table.

## The honest pairing

For a lot of homes the smart answer is both. Let the heat pump carry the whole house efficiently all season, and keep a pellet stove in the main living room for cheap, cosy heat and a bit of resilience on the coldest nights. Just be clear that the stove is comfort and backup, not a whole-home system.

## Bottom line

Choose a pellet stove if you want low-cost, hands-on heat for a main living space and pellets are cheap where you live. Choose a heat pump if you want even, whole-home heating plus cooling with almost no daily effort.
