Find a recent winter electric bill and look for **kWh used**. To estimate the cost of running a heat pump, you need to know what you would pay for the next kilowatt-hour. Dividing the total bill by kWh is a useful check, but the total can include charges that stay the same even if you use no electricity.

## Which charges count?

- **Per-kWh supply and delivery charges** usually count. Add the parts that change when you use another kWh.
- **A fixed monthly customer charge** stays on the bill whether the heat pump runs or sits idle. Include it if you are comparing total household bills.
- **Tiered or time-of-use rates** can make the next kWh cheaper or more expensive than your average. Check the rate that applies during the hours you expect to run the heat pump.
- **A seasonal heat pump tariff** only helps if your utility offers it and enrolls your account. Ask when it takes effect and which charges it changes.

## Work through one bill

Suppose your bill lists supply at **12¢/kWh**, delivery at **8¢/kWh**, and a **$15 monthly customer charge**. In this flat-rate example, the rate for adding heat pump use is **20¢/kWh**. An extra 600 kWh would cost you **$120**. The $15 charge was already on your bill. Taxes and other adjustments may change the actual amount.

If you also stop buying $160 of oil for the same rooms and period, your combined heating cost falls by $40 on those assumptions. If you still use oil for backup, subtract only the oil you expect to stop buying.
